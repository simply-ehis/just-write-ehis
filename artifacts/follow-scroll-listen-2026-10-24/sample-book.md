# The Lantern Keeper

The harbor woke before the sun did. Gulls argued over the cold pilings while the tide dragged its silver argument back out to sea. Mara wrapped both hands around the lantern pole and watched the last of the night boats nose toward the channel. She had kept this light for eleven winters, and the work had never once been boring.

Her predecessor had called the job a kind of prayer. He meant that you repeated the same small acts until they carried meaning. Trim the wick, check the glass, log the hour, walk the rail. Mara had laughed at him in her first month and agreed with him by the second winter.

The town below was still dark except for the baker's windows. A single truck moved along the waterfront with its lights on and no hurry in it. Mara could hear the bakery shutters go up, one after another, like a slow round of applause.

## The Storm That Came Without Warning

The forecast had promised nothing worse than a brisk afternoon. By noon the horizon had gone the color of a bruise and the wind was pulling spray off the tops of the waves. Mara climbed the tower twice that hour, checking the mechanism she had already checked that morning.

The radio crackled with a voice she did not know. A fishing boat was asking for the bearing to the harbor mouth, and its signal kept dissolving into static. Mara answered with the same three numbers until the voice on the other end repeated them back cleanly.

Rain arrived all at once, the way it does when the sky finally admits what it was planning. The glass streamed and the beam swung out over water that had turned the color of iron. For a moment the whole coast looked like it was being swept by a giant, patient eye.

She thought about calling the coast guard and decided against it. The boat was close enough to hear the horn if someone on board was still listening. Mara doubled the sweep rate and wrote the time in the log with a steady hand.

## What the Light Taught Her

Years later she would say the storm was the night she stopped being frightened of the work. Fear had been a companion for most of her first decade, useful at first and then simply heavy. That evening the fear packed its things and left without saying goodbye.

The boat found the channel on its own, riding the beam like a rail. Its bow swung wide, corrected, and slid between the markers with a yard to spare on either side. Mara let the horn sound three times and let herself smile at the empty room.

Afterwards the town sent a letter of thanks that nobody signed. Mara pinned it to the wall beside the older letters and added the date to the ledger. Recognition arrived so rarely in this work that she had learned to keep it in a drawer and read it on difficult mornings.

## The Long Quiet After

The next week was impossibly calm. The sea took back every wave it had borrowed and lay flat as a page under the sun. Mara walked the rail each evening and found nothing to do, which was its own kind of work.

She began keeping a second log, one nobody asked for, recording the color of the water at each hour. Fourteen shades of gray, three of green, and a blue so rare she wrote it down twice. The practice made the long shifts pass with something like dignity.

In the winter the tower would be automated, they had told her, and a camera would do the watching. Mara did not argue with the letter. She simply kept doing the work the way it had always been done, one small act repeated until it meant something.
